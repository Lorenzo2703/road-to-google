## 1) “Describe two specific goals you set for yourself and how successful you were in meeting them.”

### Goal 1 – Edge ML model for smartwatch security

Situation  
During my master’s thesis, I wanted to build a smartwatch system that could recognize emergency gestures in real time to enhance personal safety. 

Task (goal)  
My explicit goal was to design a deep‑learning model that achieved high accuracy while being small and efficient enough to run continuously on‑device. 

Action  
I chose a multi‑head 1D‑CNN architecture, designed a preprocessing pipeline (median filtering, normalization, time‑window segmentation), and iterated using TensorFlow Lite until I hit the right trade‑off between accuracy and model size. I tracked progress via validation accuracy, model size in KB, and latency per inference on the watch. 

Result  
I reached 96% accuracy with a 257.6 KB model, which met my target accuracy and fit within memory and power constraints for continuous on‑device inference, enabling discreet SOS gestures like a double fist clench. I learned how important it is to convert vague goals (“accurate and lightweight”) into measurable targets and to iterate under strict resource constraints.

### Goal 2 – Reduce manual toil with automation

Situation  
At NTTDATA, the team spent a lot of time manually processing and extracting information from candidate CVs. 

Task (goal)  
I set a personal goal to cut this manual effort significantly by introducing automation, even though it wasn’t an explicit requirement of my backend role. 

Action  
I implemented a CV scraping and parsing tool that read resumes, extracted structured data fields, and integrated them into existing systems. I measured progress by the number of CVs processed per hour and the reduction in manual steps per CV. 

Result  
The team’s throughput increased and manual processing time per CV dropped substantially, freeing engineers to focus on higher‑value tasks. I learned that identifying and eliminating operational toil can have outsized impact, and I’ve carried that mindset into later projects like the PREVENT‑PCP evaluation framework.

## 2) “Tell me about a time when you failed to meet a deadline. What did you fail to do? What did you learn?”

Situation  
In the early phases of my smartwatch gesture‑recognition work, I initially planned an ambitious set of gestures and model complexity to deliver by a specific internal milestone. 

Task  
My goal was to support a broad gesture set with a highly sophisticated model by the milestone date, while still running everything on‑device. 

Action  
As I implemented the model, I realized that memory and latency constraints on the watch were tighter than I had planned for, and integrating all targeted gestures into a single architecture became much harder than expected. I missed my initial internal milestone for the full gesture set and had to scale back. 

Result / root cause and learning  
The root cause was optimistic scoping without enough early profiling on real hardware. I adjusted by narrowing the supported gestures to the most critical emergency ones, simplifying the architecture, and building hardware profiling and constraint checks into my planning for subsequent iterations. Since then, in both academic and industry projects, I set intermediate checkpoints, validate assumptions on real systems early, and explicitly bake constraints into my design goals to avoid similar slips.

## 3) Hypothetical: “Imagine you are in charge of organizing the grand opening of a new Google office in Bangalore, India. What steps would you take to plan this event?”

You can answer this like a structured engineering/design problem.

1. Clarify objectives and success metrics  
I would start by clarifying the primary objectives: for example, showcasing Google’s presence in Bangalore, strengthening relationships with local partners, giving employees a sense of pride, and generating positive press. I’d define success metrics such as attendance numbers across key stakeholder groups, post‑event satisfaction surveys, press coverage quality, and whether we hit budget and schedule.

2. Identify stakeholders and invitees  
Next, I’d map stakeholders: Google leadership and employees in India, local and regional leadership, key partners, potential customers, community organizations, and relevant government or university representatives. I’d work with internal comms and local HR/leadership to define invite lists and messaging tailored to each group.

3. High‑level plan and timeline  
I’d draft a timeline backwards from the event date: venue readiness checks, approvals, vendor selection, internal communication, and dry‑runs. In parallel, I’d define workstreams with owners: logistics, program/content, communications/PR, and risk/contingency planning.

4. Logistics: location, setup, and operations  
On logistics, I’d confirm the office space that will be showcased (e.g., certain floors, meeting rooms, demo areas) and ensure safety and accessibility. I’d coordinate with facilities for seating, AV setup, signage, catering, registration flow, and wayfinding so that arrival and movement through the space are smooth.

5. Program and content  
For the program, I’d propose a clear arc: a short opening by Google leadership, a talk about Google’s mission and local impact, product or tech demos relevant to the Bangalore ecosystem, and time for networking. I’d involve local teams in selecting demos that highlight real work done in Bangalore rather than generic “global” content.

6. Communication and branding  
I’d work with comms/PR to craft invitations, a landing page, and press materials that explain why Google is investing in Bangalore and how this office fits into that story. I’d plan internal communication so employees know what to expect, how they can participate, and what behavior is expected during the event.

7. Risk management and contingency plans  
I’d identify risks—over‑capacity, technical issues with AV, last‑minute speaker cancellations—and plan mitigations such as overflow spaces, pre‑event AV checks, backup speakers, and a clear point
